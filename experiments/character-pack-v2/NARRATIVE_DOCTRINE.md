# Character Pack v2 — Narrative Doctrine

Status: **DRAFT / STORY + DESIGN RULES**

Tracked by GitHub Issue #15.

## Core story rule

The characters do not begin by believing they have superpowers.

Their abilities are discovered only when a real problem demands the deeper function of what they already are.

The story can call those abilities powers, but they are not arbitrary magic:

- Pencil creates.
- Eraser corrects.
- Paper Clip connects.
- Highlighter emphasizes.
- Ruler measures.
- Sticky Note remembers.
- Cursor chooses.
- Bookmark marks and returns.
- Key unlocks and grants access.
- Ink Bottle records and preserves through writing.
- Mobi asks, connects, and interfaces with AI.
- Percy holds, groups, contains, and protects what belongs together.

## Discovery rule

Do not explain a power before it matters.

Build the circumstance first. Let the ordinary object-function become unexpectedly essential. The character and the audience discover its importance together.

A character may initially think their normal function is boring, decorative, embarrassing, or less useful than somebody else's. The story proves otherwise through action.

## Percy

Percy can begin elegant and visibly beautiful while privately believing parentheses are not especially important. She may think she mostly sits around words, keeps things tidy, and looks pretty.

Then something starts coming apart: friends are separated, related clues are split up, or a solution cannot stay together long enough to be understood.

Percy's paired nature becomes the answer. She holds related things together, contains what must not scatter, and protects the relationship between the pieces.

Her line **"I've got you."** should arrive after that discovery, not as empty branding.

Percy is never a generic forcefield character. Her action must remain recognizably parenthetical: pairing, grouping, containment, relationship.

## Mobi

Mobi is a mobile phone and the group's AI interface.

The group can ask Mobi anything. That does not mean Mobi can correctly answer everything.

Mobi may:
- answer from available evidence,
- distinguish observation from inference,
- say that something is unknown,
- ask the group to gather another fact,
- connect information that the others have produced.

Mobi may not:
- invent missing facts,
- know off-screen events without a source,
- solve every mystery alone,
- replace the native functions of the other characters.

This keeps Mobi powerful without turning him into a story-ending oracle.

## Writing test

For any character-power scene, ask:

1. What ordinary object-function is being dramatized?
2. Why is that function suddenly necessary here?
3. Could another character solve it just as easily? If yes, the scene is probably not character-specific enough.
4. Did the story create the need before revealing the ability?
5. Does the character understand something new about their own value afterward?

The goal is not "objects with random superpowers." The goal is **ordinary functions becoming extraordinary when they are exactly what the moment requires.**
