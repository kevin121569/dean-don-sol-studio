# LIVING LEARNING OBJECTS — MINIMAL ILLUSTRATED PRODUCTION DOCTRINE v1

Status: **PRODUCTION-DIRECTION LOCK / REPLACES 3D-MASCOT ASPIRATION**  
Tracked by GitHub Issue #23 / PR #24.

## Core decision

Living Learning Objects is not a Pixar-style 3D character series.

The series is an **illustrated, voice-driven, limited-animation story world** built around instantly readable objects and symbols with strong personalities.

The object or symbol is the character.

Bodies, limbs, clothes, props, and facial details exist only to support personality and performance. They must never become the main visual identity or create a production burden that prevents episodes from being finished.

**Production sentence:** If a character can be understood from the object/symbol silhouette plus voice and expression, the design is doing its job.

## Why this is the locked direction

This style protects all three goals at once:

1. **Story first** — writing, voice, timing, humor, wisdom, and emotional truth carry the episode.
2. **Character clarity** — Percy remains parentheses, Clipper remains a paper clip, Hue remains a highlighter, Mobi remains a phone.
3. **Affordable repeatability** — episodes can be produced with drawings, pose changes, mouth/eye swaps, simple camera moves, layered parallax, limited limb motion, and occasional controlled transformations.

No episode should require expensive animation simply to prove that the world matters.

## Visual identity hierarchy

Use this order when deciding what may change:

1. **native object/symbol silhouette** — never sacrificed,
2. **face / expression system** — primary acting surface,
3. **voice / dialogue / timing** — primary personality engine,
4. **simple limbs / stick body** — optional support,
5. **clothing / accessories** — optional personality layer,
6. **background detail / cinematic polish** — last priority.

Clothes may be funny, elegant, old-fashioned, messy, formal, seasonal, or story-specific. They do not redefine what the character is.

## Body doctrine

Bodies are intentionally simple.

Allowed:
- stick arms and legs,
- wire-like limbs,
- simple glove/hand shapes when a gesture needs clarity,
- tiny shoes or feet,
- simple torso line or minimal body connector when necessary,
- no body at all for shots where the object itself can perform the action,
- clothing attached to the simple body or object when it helps characterize the moment.

Not allowed as a default:
- detailed human anatomy,
- fully modeled mascot bodies,
- fur simulation,
- realistic cloth simulation,
- expensive deformation rigs,
- complex walk-cycle dependence,
- body designs that make the object/symbol secondary.

If the body takes longer to understand than the object, simplify it.

## Face doctrine

Faces carry performance.

Prefer:
- expressive eyes,
- eyebrows when useful,
- simple mouths,
- a small number of reusable expression states,
- pose and timing over facial rendering complexity.

A character should remain recognizable even if the face is reduced to eyes + mouth.

## Drawing style

Target look:
- hand-drawn or hand-drawn-adjacent,
- black-and-white or limited-color friendly,
- clean readable silhouette,
- simple ink / pencil / marker line,
- optional flat color fills,
- selective color accents for meaning,
- no dependency on realism.

Preferred production spectrum:

### Mode A — black-and-white base
Ink/pencil-style drawings with expressive faces and minimal bodies.

### Mode B — black-and-white plus selective color
Mostly monochrome frames with character/evidence accents.

### Mode C — flat illustrated color
Simple repeatable fills, still clearly drawn and non-3D.

Any episode may use one of these, but all must feel like the same illustrated universe.

## Color doctrine

Color is optional storytelling information, not production obligation.

Use color when it helps:
- distinguish a character,
- focus attention,
- support Hue's function,
- mark evidence,
- signal emotional emphasis,
- make a special story beat readable.

Do not add color merely because a frame feels empty.

Black-and-white must remain a valid fallback production mode for every episode.

## Clothing doctrine

Characters may wear clothes and accessories.

Examples:
- Percy can wear a tiny crown, bow, dress suggestion, scarf, or elegant accessory,
- Clipper can wear a vest, little hat, spectacles, or grandfatherly accessory while remaining visibly a paper clip,
- Hue can wear a cap/band or expressive accessory without ceasing to be a highlighter,
- Mobi may use a case, sticker, strap, or simple prop without becoming a robot.

Rules:
- clothing never hides the native object/symbol silhouette,
- wardrobe changes do not require new anatomy,
- clothes must be drawable with a few repeatable shapes,
- do not create costume detail that becomes harder to animate than the character.

## Percy lock under this doctrine

Percy is one character represented by two coordinated parenthesis halves.

She is not a yellow mascot, human girl, princess body, pair of twins, brackets, braces, or glowing magical object.

Her acting is carried by:
- eye placement,
- mouth/expression,
- relative position of her two halves,
- simple stick limbs only when useful,
- optional elegant clothing/accessories that do not hide the parenthesis shapes,
- voice and timing.

Her first discovery remains physically literal:
- she does not grow,
- she does not stretch,
- she does not cast a forcefield,
- she does not remote-grab,
- her two normal-size halves separate and take position around an already-related set,
- direct physical containment keeps the set together.

The emotional idea is simple:

**Percy thinks she is only pretty. Then she discovers that the shape she thought was decorative can matter.**

Beauty is not rejected. Usefulness is added to her self-understanding.

## Clipper lock under this doctrine

Clipper is a paper clip.

He may have:
- simple eyes/face,
- stick limbs,
- grandfather clothes/accessories,
- a permanent slight bend,
- an old, warm, practical voice.

He may not become:
- a human grandfather,
- a man wearing a paper-clip costume,
- a generic old-person mascot.

His paper-clip geometry must remain visible and physically meaningful when he fastens things.

## Hue lock under this doctrine

Hue is a highlighter.

He may be funny, energetic, dramatic, worried, proud, or ridiculous.

His personality must not turn him into a question-mark creature or generic mascot.

His visual identity remains a real highlighter shape with expressive face and optional simple limbs/accessories.

## Mobi lock under this doctrine

Mobi is a mobile phone.

He is not a robot mascot.

His face may live on the screen. Simple stick limbs may be added when a gesture is needed.

He may use screen expressions, icons, or simple text derived from shared story evidence.

He does not gain robot anatomy, hovering parts, antenna personality, or sci-fi hardware merely to look more animated.

## Animation doctrine

The target is **limited animation with strong editorial rhythm**.

Primary tools:
- pose-to-pose changes,
- eye blinks,
- mouth swaps,
- simple stick-arm gestures,
- small body/object bobs,
- pan/zoom across illustrations,
- parallax layers,
- foreground/background separation,
- simple object motion,
- scene cuts timed to voice and music,
- occasional high-value transformation shots.

A shot does not need continuous motion to feel alive.

Stillness can carry emotion.

## Production-budget law

**If a scene requires expensive animation to communicate the story, redesign the scene.**

Prefer:
- one strong pose over twelve weak frames,
- one clean silhouette over complicated acting,
- one memorable line over visual spectacle,
- sound design over unnecessary movement,
- an editorial cut over a complex camera move,
- selective motion over constant motion.

The project succeeds by finishing stories, not by winning a rendering contest.

## Voice doctrine

Voice is one of the main animation systems.

Character differentiation should come heavily from:
- cadence,
- confidence,
- humor,
- pauses,
- age impression,
- attitude,
- emotional restraint or exuberance.

A great vocal performance can make a mostly still drawing feel fully alive.

## Wisdom / knowledge doctrine

The stories may teach:
- wisdom,
- reasoning,
- evidence,
- uncertainty,
- revision,
- cooperation,
- self-worth,
- limitations,
- consequences,
- attention,
- memory,
- decision-making,
- better questions.

But the series does not stop to become a worksheet.

The character lives through the lesson before the audience names it.

## Reuse doctrine

Every design must support reuse.

For each recurring character, production should maintain:
- one canonical silhouette,
- one front reference,
- one three-quarter reference,
- 6–10 reusable expressions,
- 4–6 reusable hand/arm gesture states if limbs are used,
- one neutral standing pose,
- one speaking pose,
- one thinking pose,
- one emotional-closeup pose,
- small wardrobe overlays rather than redrawing the body.

This is the asset economy that makes many episodes possible.

## Background doctrine

Backgrounds may be more atmospheric than characters, but they should remain illustration-friendly.

Prefer:
- reusable rooms,
- reusable tables/trays/shelves,
- simple perspective,
- layered still backgrounds,
- limited moving machinery,
- location changes achieved through set dressing and camera crops.

Do not build a new expensive environment for every episode unless the story truly needs it.

## AI-generation doctrine

Generative image/video tools are assistants, not canon authorities.

Reject any generation that:
- converts objects into human/animal mascots,
- hides the native object/symbol,
- adds expensive anatomy the production cannot maintain,
- changes Percy into one solid character body,
- turns Clipper into a human grandfather,
- turns Hue into a question mark or creature,
- turns Mobi into a robot,
- introduces arbitrary magic or glowing powers,
- makes character consistency depend on a lucky one-off render.

A simpler repeatable design outranks a prettier inconsistent one.

## Acceptance test

Before a design is approved, ask:

1. Can a child identify the original object/symbol immediately?
2. Can we redraw/reuse this character quickly?
3. Can the face carry emotion without complex anatomy?
4. Can clothes/accessories be swapped without rebuilding the character?
5. Can the episode work with mostly still drawings plus limited motion?
6. Would black-and-white still work?
7. Does the design strengthen personality rather than merely add rendering detail?
8. Can this character survive 30 episodes without bankrupting production time?

If the answer to #8 is no, simplify.

## Current production direction

**LOCKED:** Living Learning Objects proceeds as a low-cost illustrated limited-animation series, character-first and voice-driven, with simple bodies and optional clothes/accessories.

3D mascot-style boards are concept references only and are not production canon.

Next proof: create Percy alone in this illustrated language and verify that her personality reads before expanding the rest of the cast.
