# LIVING LEARNING OBJECTS — STORY BIBLE v1

Status: **DRAFT STORY LOCK / NOT PRODUCTION SCRIPT**

Tracked by GitHub Issue #23.

## Series promise

A group of ordinary learning objects slowly discovers that the thing each of them was made to do can become extraordinary when the right problem appears.

They do not begin as superheroes. They do not know a list of powers. They do not receive abilities from an outside magical source.

The story creates a need first. The object-function becomes essential second. The character understands something new about their own value third.

**Core sentence:** Ordinary functions become extraordinary when they are exactly what the moment requires.

## Audience and tone

Core audience: **roughly ages 7–11**, written so an older sibling or parent can enjoy watching or reading with them.

Tone:
- warm,
- funny,
- curious,
- visually magical without relying on arbitrary magic,
- emotionally sincere without becoming preachy,
- smart enough to let children notice the answer before a character sometimes does.

The world should reward:
- observation,
- asking better questions,
- changing your mind when evidence changes,
- noticing what another person or tool is good at,
- admitting uncertainty,
- cooperation without flattening everybody into the same skill set.

The stories should never feel like disguised vocabulary worksheets. The lesson is carried by the adventure.

## World rule

Every character remains recognizably the object or symbol they already are.

Characterization may enrich the object. It may not disguise it.

A pencil does not become a wizard with a pencil costume. A paper clip does not shoot unrelated energy beams. Parentheses do not become a generic forcefield.

The extraordinary moment must still make sense if described with the ordinary verb:
- Pencil **creates**.
- Eraser **corrects**.
- Clipper **connects / fastens**.
- Hue **emphasizes / focuses attention**.
- Ruler **measures**.
- Sticky Note **remembers**.
- Cursor **chooses**.
- Bookmark **marks / returns**.
- Key **unlocks / grants access**.
- Ink Bottle **records / preserves through writing**.
- Mobi **asks / connects / interfaces with AI**.
- Percy **groups / contains / protects relationships**.

If an action cannot be explained by the native verb, it probably does not belong to that character.

## Power-discovery law

### 1. Need before ability
The audience must see the problem before seeing the extraordinary use of a function.

### 2. No advance power list
No mentor speech explains what everyone will someday do.

### 3. Discovery is personal
The character should understand something new about their own worth after the first major use.

### 4. Native function only
No arbitrary magic powers.

### 5. Not everybody discovers at once
A story may feature many characters using ordinary skills, but a first-discovery episode should belong primarily to one character.

### 6. Problems must remain problems
Mobi cannot answer away the story. Key cannot open every obstacle. Eraser cannot erase consequences. Percy cannot contain anything merely because containment would be convenient.

## Character roster

### Percy — Parenthesis Princess

**Form:** one character represented by the pair `( )`.

**Native function:** grouping, relationship, containment.

**Emotional starting point:** Percy is elegant and pretty and knows it. What she does not know is whether being parentheses matters very much.

She sees Pencil make things. Eraser fixes things. Clipper physically keeps things together. Key opens things. Percy can privately wonder whether she mostly stands around other people’s work and makes it look tidy.

**Deeper discovery:** Percy can hold related things together when separation would destroy the relationship between them. She can contain what must remain together long enough for meaning, safety, or understanding to survive.

**Not allowed:** generic shields, forcefields, telekinesis, wrapping anything regardless of relationship.

**Signature line:** **“I’ve got you.”**

The line should be earned in action before it becomes a recurring phrase.

### Clipper — the Paper Clip

**Relationship:** Percy’s grandpa — working canon.

**Native function:** physically fastening pieces that already belong together.

**Personality:** practical, old-school, slightly bent, patient, difficult to impress with flashy presentation.

Clipper has spent a lifetime doing an unglamorous job well. He does not need to know Percy’s future ability. His value in the story is that he already understands something Percy has not yet learned: keeping things together can matter enormously even when nobody applauds it.

He never explains Percy to herself.

**Percy distinction:**
- Clipper fastens pieces.
- Percy protects the relationship between pieces.

Possible Grandpa humor after Percy’s first discovery:

> “Huh. Knew you were good for more than decoration.”

Use only if the scene earns it; the emotional beat is pride, not insult.

### Hue the Highlighter

**Native function:** emphasizing what matters.

**Personality possibility:** energetic, observant, tempted to make everything important.

Hue’s lesson is that standing out is not the same as being useful. Highlighting everything is the same as highlighting nothing.

Color can change when color has meaning:
- fact,
- question,
- contradiction,
- evidence to verify.

Never use rainbow cycling as personality wallpaper.

### Pencil

**Native function:** creating / drafting.

Pencil can make a first attempt visible. Pencil should be comfortable with unfinished ideas because a draft is allowed to change.

### Eraser

**Native function:** correcting / revising.

Eraser should not represent shame or punishment. Correction is part of making something better.

A mistake that can be edited is not a catastrophe.

### Ruler

**Native function:** measuring / comparing / aligning.

Ruler cares about what can actually be compared. A useful recurring joke is that Ruler becomes annoyed when someone asks for precision about something nobody measured.

### Sticky Note

**Native function:** remembering one useful thing.

Sticky Note should preserve short deterministic reminders, not magically reconstruct the past.

Memory can be useful precisely because it is limited and deliberate.

### Cursor

**Native function:** choosing.

Cursor indicates a real decision; Cursor does not make the decision secretly for everyone else.

The underlying choice must remain understandable without Cursor.

### Bookmark

**Native function:** marking a place and returning to it.

Bookmark is useful when progress does not mean starting over.

### Key

**Native function:** unlocking access when the correct lock and conditions exist.

Key is mechanical, not magical. A locked thing can remain locked if Key does not belong there.

### Ink Bottle

**Native function:** recording / preserving through writing.

Ink Bottle belongs to the quieter, more literary side of the world. Ink can make something last, but writing something down does not automatically make it true.

### Mobi — mobile AI interface

**Form:** visibly a mobile phone.

**Native function:** receives questions, connects information, communicates AI responses.

Mobi is powerful because Mobi can help the group think, not because Mobi knows the answer to every plot.

Mobi may say:
- “Here is what we observed.”
- “That is a guess, not a fact.”
- “I don’t know yet.”
- “We need one more observation.”
- “These two facts may be related, but we have not proved it.”

Mobi may not:
- know off-screen events without evidence,
- invent missing facts,
- identify the culprit because the script needs to end,
- replace Hue’s focus, Ruler’s measurement, Percy’s containment, Clipper’s connection, or another character’s native function.

A good Mobi answer should often create the **next useful question** rather than close the mystery.

## Relationship engine

The cast works because their functions overlap just enough to cause misunderstandings but differ enough that no character is redundant.

Useful relationship contrasts:

- Percy vs. Clipper: contain/protect relationship vs. fasten pieces.
- Hue vs. Mobi: what deserves attention vs. what information is available.
- Pencil vs. Eraser: first attempt vs. revision.
- Ruler vs. intuition: measured difference vs. “it looks bigger.”
- Sticky Note vs. Mobi: deliberately remembered instruction vs. queried information.
- Cursor vs. Mobi: choosing an action vs. receiving information about options.
- Bookmark vs. Eraser: return to earlier work vs. change earlier work.

Stories should exploit these differences.

## PILOT — “I’VE GOT YOU”

### Pilot purpose

The pilot proves the series engine by making Percy the first character to discover a deeper ability.

Everybody else uses ordinary functions. Percy gets the extraordinary moment.

### Percy’s emotional question

**“What good am I if all I do is sit around things other people made?”**

The plot should answer this without a lecture.

### Opening state

The group is assembling a set of related clue cards / story pieces / instructions for a shared task.

Pencil drafts labels.
Eraser fixes a mistake.
Clipper fastens pages that belong together.
Hue marks one important instruction.
Ruler checks that two pieces actually match.
Sticky Note keeps one short reminder.
Cursor helps choose which route to try.
Mobi answers questions about what is known and what is still missing.

Percy watches everyone being obviously useful.

She is visually elegant and may receive a harmless compliment for looking pretty. The compliment accidentally deepens her worry: maybe looking pretty is all she contributes.

### Inciting problem

A disruption separates the related pieces.

This should not be a villain attack merely to create action. A believable story-world event is stronger: a gust through an open window, a moving conveyor, a collapsing stack, a sorting machine, a page-transfer mechanism, or another physical process that sends the pieces into different paths.

The key condition: **the pieces only make sense together.**

### First attempts

Clipper can fasten two physical pieces, but cannot reach or hold the whole relationship at once.

Hue can identify the piece containing the key instruction, but highlighting the right clue does not reunite the set.

Ruler can prove which edges / sizes correspond, but measurement does not stop separation.

Mobi can say what is known and what remains unknown. Crucially, Mobi cannot simply tell them where every missing piece is.

Example Mobi logic:

> “I know these three belong to the same sequence. I do not know where the fourth one went.”

That answer helps without solving the story.

### Midpoint pressure

The group recovers most of the pieces, but they cannot keep the related set together long enough to complete the task.

Percy tries to help in ordinary ways and fails or is gently moved aside because nobody can immediately think of what parentheses are supposed to do in a physical crisis.

Her insecurity becomes explicit without turning into a speech about self-esteem.

Possible quiet Percy line:

> “Everybody does something.”

Clipper can answer simply:

> “So do you.”

He does not explain further because he genuinely may not know how her function will manifest.

### Crisis

The final related pieces begin separating again, this time in a way Clipper cannot physically fasten quickly enough.

The group calls the pieces by relationship — not merely by location:
- “Those two belong with this one.”
- “Don’t let the question get separated from its answer.”
- “We need the whole set.”

Percy reacts to the **relationship**.

### Discovery moment

Her two parentheses move apart, surround what belongs together, and close around the set.

No glow.
No energy bubble.
No unrelated magic.

The visual should still unmistakably be parentheses doing what parentheses do.

The related pieces stop scattering because they are now being held as one group.

Percy is as surprised as everyone else.

Then she sees one friend / piece still slipping away.

She closes the pair just enough and says, for the first time:

**“I’ve got you.”**

That is the pilot’s emotional and visual payoff.

### Resolution

With the related set held together, the other characters can finally do their jobs:
- Hue focuses the decisive clue.
- Ruler confirms the comparison.
- Pencil completes the missing mark or label.
- Eraser corrects the wrong assumption.
- Cursor chooses the final route.
- Mobi summarizes what is now supported by evidence.

Percy did not solve everything. She made it possible for the group to solve it together.

### Grandpa payoff

Clipper recognizes the family resemblance before Percy fully understands it.

Preferred emotional beat: a small smile / physical straightening / proud glance rather than exposition.

Optional line:

> “Huh. Knew you were good for more than decoration.”

Percy can glare for half a second, then smile.

### End image

Earlier Percy stood beside the work.

Now the group naturally leaves a space for her **around** the thing they are building.

Her visual position itself communicates that she belongs.

## Pilot anti-rules

Do not:
- reveal everybody’s superpower,
- have Mobi identify the answer from nowhere,
- give Percy a generic shield,
- let Clipper explain Percy’s ability before she discovers it,
- turn Percy’s sadness into melodrama,
- solve the episode with a motivational speech,
- make beauty the thing Percy must reject; she can enjoy being pretty and still discover she is useful,
- make another character incompetent so Percy can shine.

## Season engine

Each episode asks:

1. What ordinary function has the group underestimated?
2. What problem naturally makes that function essential?
3. What wrong approach seems reasonable first?
4. What evidence changes the group’s understanding?
5. Who learns something about their value or limitation?
6. What remains true after the episode that can matter later?

### Example discovery episodes

**Hue — “Too Much Important”**  
Everything is highlighted. Nothing stands out. Hue learns that focus requires choosing what *not* to emphasize.

**Eraser — “The Wrong Right Answer”**  
The group becomes attached to a beautiful solution that evidence disproves. Eraser discovers that correction can save good work rather than destroy it.

**Ruler — “Looks Bigger”**  
Two paths look unequal. Ruler discovers that measurement can settle an argument nobody can solve by confidence.

**Sticky Note — “Don’t Forget the One Thing”**  
The group gathers too much information and loses a tiny instruction that matters later. Sticky learns that remembering everything is not the same as remembering what matters.

**Cursor — “You Choose”**  
Everyone wants Cursor to decide for them. Cursor learns that indicating a choice is different from owning someone else’s decision.

**Mobi — “Unknown”**  
The group assumes AI should know. Mobi’s meaningful act is refusing to invent an answer and directing them toward the observation they still need.

**Clipper — “Loose Pages”**  
Clipper’s ordinary, old-fashioned function prevents a chain of evidence from being scattered. The episode can reveal why Percy admires him even while teasing him for being ancient.

## Long-form emotional arc

The deeper season idea is not “everyone learns they are secretly powerful.”

It is:

**value depends on fit between a person/tool and the problem — not on who looks most impressive in isolation.**

Characters can still fail. A native function can be the wrong tool for a problem. Mobi can be uncertain. Hue can emphasize the wrong thing. Ruler can precisely measure something irrelevant. Pencil can create a bad draft. Eraser can remove something worth keeping.

Growth means learning when and how to use what you are good at.

## Game and puzzle hooks

The character functions translate naturally into interactive mechanics without breaking story canon:

- Percy: group related evidence into sets.
- Clipper: connect pages / clues already shown to belong together.
- Hue: highlight the sentence that actually answers a question.
- Ruler: compare scale, order, distance, or measurable differences.
- Sticky Note: choose one clue to carry forward between scenes.
- Cursor: make explicit route / hypothesis choices.
- Bookmark: mark a location to revisit after new evidence appears.
- Key: unlock only after the correct prerequisite is met.
- Eraser: revise an earlier answer after contradictory evidence.
- Pencil: create a hypothesis / label / missing piece.
- Mobi: ask a constrained question and receive observation / inference / unknown classification.
- Ink Bottle: record the final supported account of what happened.

A game should never let the character mechanic reveal information the story has not earned.

## Reading-activity hooks

Every episode can support three optional reader prompts:

**What happened?** — observation  
**What do you think it means?** — inference  
**What would you need to know next?** — better question

These should remain optional and playful rather than stopping the story for a lesson.

## Visual-story rule

The first time a deeper function appears, the visual should be clearer than the dialogue.

A child should be able to understand what happened before a character names it.

That is especially important for Percy:

`( what belongs together )`

is the explanation.

## Canon hierarchy

When future scripts conflict, use this priority:

1. native object/symbol function,
2. locked character identity,
3. established relationship rules,
4. current story circumstance,
5. joke / spectacle.

A joke or visual effect never outranks character logic.
