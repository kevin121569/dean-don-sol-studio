# LIVING LEARNING OBJECTS — REFERENCE STYLE LOCK v1

Status: **PRODUCTION STYLE LOCK / LOW-COST ILLUSTRATED ANIMATION**
Tracked by GitHub Issue #23 / PR #24.

## Reference direction

The production should borrow the **economy and object-character language of early black-and-white theatrical cartoons and illustrated storybooks**, not their full animation density.

The target is:

- hand-drawn / ink-line appearance,
- simple recognizable object or symbol silhouettes,
- black-and-white or warm grayscale base,
- selective spot color only when useful,
- minimal bodies and limbs,
- optional clothing/accessories for personality,
- strong faces and voices,
- limited pose changes,
- held drawings, pans, cuts, blinks, eye shifts, mouth swaps, and small gestures,
- occasional larger motion only when the story absolutely earns it.

We do **not** target Pixar-style modeling, full 3D acting, constant squash-and-stretch, dense frame-by-frame movement, or expensive character rigs.

## Character-first rule

The object/symbol identity is the character. The body is support.

A character may wear clothing, shoes, glasses, hats, scarves, bags, or other accessories when those choices strengthen personality. These additions must never obscure the underlying object/symbol silhouette.

If a character can no longer be identified instantly without the costume, the design has drifted.

## Percy — joined face / separated function

Percy is one person represented by a pair of parentheses.

### Normal state

When her two halves stand together, they visually resolve into **one complete face**.

Recommended construction:

- one eye/eyebrow/cheek cue belongs primarily to the left half,
- the matching eye/eyebrow/cheek cue belongs primarily to the right half,
- the mouth is composed so the two halves together read as one face,
- the audience perceives one personality, not twins.

The emotional effect may loosely evoke conjoined / joined twins visually, but Percy is canonically **one character with two coordinated halves**.

### Working / separated state

When Percy needs to contain a related set, her two halves separate spatially.

The face divides with the halves instead of transforming into two independent people.

Important:

- no duplicate Percy,
- no two separate consciousnesses,
- no independent personalities for left/right halves,
- no growth, stretching, or shape morphing,
- no magical forcefield,
- one voice continues to represent Percy,
- lip-sync may be minimal or omitted while separated; expression can be carried by eyes, brows, timing, and voice.

This is intentionally production-cheap: the split is a compositional move, not a transformation-heavy effect.

## Motion budget doctrine

The show should behave more like an illustrated motion story than a fully animated theatrical short.

Default movement hierarchy:

1. static drawing + voice,
2. eye movement / blink,
3. mouth swap,
4. small hand/limb gesture,
5. camera pan / push / crop change,
6. prop movement,
7. pose swap,
8. short character translation,
9. rare larger action beat.

If an episode depends on level 9 repeatedly, redesign the episode.

## Old-cartoon reference — what to borrow

Borrow:

- literal objects becoming characters,
- black-line clarity,
- simple readable silhouettes,
- clothing/accessories as fast personality shorthand,
- expressive faces,
- visual jokes built from the object’s real function,
- willingness to let a drawing be strange, charming, and symbolic.

Do not borrow by default:

- nonstop rubber-hose motion,
- constant body deformation,
- highly labor-intensive frame-by-frame acting,
- frantic theatrical pacing,
- visual noise that competes with dialogue.

## Backgrounds

Backgrounds may be:

- pencil/ink drawings,
- lightly shaded monochrome plates,
- reused perspective layouts,
- selectively colored only around important information.

They do not need full animated depth.

A still background with one moving prop or camera pan is acceptable production language.

## Voice-first storytelling

The main production value is:

- writing,
- character voice,
- timing,
- music/sound,
- composition,
- personality.

Animation exists to clarify and support those things.

A strong line delivered over a nearly still drawing is valid finished storytelling.

## Production test

Before approving a design or shot, ask:

1. Can a child identify the character from the object/symbol alone?
2. Can this shot work with mostly static art and voice?
3. Does clothing add personality rather than animation burden?
4. Can the expression be sold with eyes/brows/mouth swaps instead of full-body acting?
5. Could we reproduce this character consistently for dozens of episodes?
6. Does the scene still work without expensive motion?

If not, simplify.

## Percy proof target

The next visual proof should show only:

- Percy joined in her normal one-face state,
- Percy separated in her working state,
- the same normal-size halves in both states,
- minimal stick/wire limbs,
- optional simple shoes or one clothing accent,
- primarily black-and-white / ink drawing,
- at most one restrained spot color,
- no glossy 3D rendering,
- no mascot body mass,
- no Pixar-style volume,
- no complex animation assumptions.

This proof must be visually compelling as a drawing before any animation is considered.
